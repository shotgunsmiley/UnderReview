The Glass Vault and the Company Safe

What ten crypto gambling sites actually do with your money — written for someone who has used an online casino maybe once, lost forty dollars, and never touched a crypto wallet.

10 platforms reviewed · 5 technical criteria · Sources & references included · Reading time: ~20 minutes

Part One: How I Got Here

I am not a gambler. I want to establish that first, because everything below depends on it.

I play a puzzle game on my phone. I've bought maybe two lottery tickets in my life. My experience with casinos was a single trip to one on a weekend away, where I lost $40 on a slot machine shaped like a leprechaun and felt bad about it for a day. That is my entire résumé.

So when I tell you I ended up with money in a crypto casino, I want you to know exactly how ordinary the path was.

It started with an ad. You've probably seen the same one: a guy in a nice car, a screenshot of a balance with too many zeros, and text that says "PROVABLY FAIR" and "INSTANT WITHDRAWALS." I scrolled past it maybe a dozen times. Then one night I was bored, the ad came up again, and I thought: what actually is this?

I signed up. It took about ninety seconds. No ID, no phone call, nothing. Just an email address.

I happened to have about $50 in crypto sitting in a wallet — a friend had paid me back for a group gift that way a few months earlier, and I'd never bothered to convert it. The site told me to send it to an address. I copied, pasted, sent. Two minutes later my balance showed $50.00. It felt, honestly, like magic. I'd expected the whole thing to be incomprehensible.

I played a dice game, because it was the one game where I could figure out the rules in under a minute. I bet $5. I won. I bet $10. I won. I bet $20 and lost. I bet $20 and won. Somewhere in there the number on my screen went from $50 to $180 and I remember thinking, very clearly: this is absurd, I should stop now.

So I stopped. And I clicked withdraw.

And that is where the story actually begins, because the withdrawal did not arrive. Instead I got a message: "Your withdrawal is under review."

I waited. I checked the site the next morning. Still under review. I found a support chat, and a person (or a chat window pretending to be a person) told me that to withdraw, I first had to meet the wagering requirements. The what, I said. The wagering requirements, they said. I needed to bet my deposit a certain number of times before the balance was really mine to take out. It was written in the terms. I had not read the terms. I had clicked "I agree" the way I click "I agree" on everything.

So I bet more. Not because I wanted to — because I had to, to get my own money back. I lost most of it. By the time I cleared the requirement my $180 was about $40, and when I finally withdrew, $40 is what arrived.

I want to be careful here about what I'm blaming. Nobody stole from me. Everything that happened was disclosed, in writing, in a document I chose not to read. That's on me. But I learned something that night that I have not stopped thinking about: the money I saw on my screen was never actually mine. It was a number the company was keeping for me, and the conditions attached to it were theirs to set.

Fine. Lesson learned. I closed the tab and assumed I was done with this whole world.

Except a few weeks later I read a comment somewhere — a forum, I don't remember — where someone mentioned a different kind of site. Not a casino, they said. A "protocol." A thing where the money went into a public pot that anyone could look at, and when you won, it just... paid you.

I did not understand this at all. But I was curious, so I tried it. I put $10 in. I played. I won a little. I clicked withdraw.

It arrived in my wallet in about forty seconds. No review. No email. No support ticket. No terms I hadn't read. Just: the money left the pot and arrived in my account.

That confused me more than the friction had. I didn't understand why one site made me wait two days and the other didn't wait at all. So I started reading — Reddit threads, documentation pages written for people who already knew the vocabulary, block explorers that looked like the Matrix.

It took me about three weeks to figure out the sentence that made everything click, and I'm going to give it to you now, right at the top, because it's the whole article:

The site that made me wait was holding my money. The site that paid me instantly was never holding it at all.

The entire difference, in one sentence

That's it. That's the entire difference. Everything else — the graphics, the bonuses, the VIP programs, the "provably fair" badges, the number of games — is decoration on top of that one structural fact, and almost nobody explains it to beginners because almost everybody in this space is trying to sell you something.

So this is written for the version of me from three weeks ago. Someone with an internet connection, a mild curiosity, and no idea what any of the words mean. I've since gone through ten of these sites — six that work the way that second one did, and four of the biggest names in the business, including a couple I had absolutely heard of — and I want to show you what I found.

None of it requires you to understand blockchain, or crypto, or finance. It requires you to understand the difference between a vault with glass walls and a safe you can't see into.

Part Two: The Two Setups

Before we look at the ten sites, you need two ideas. That's all. Two.

Setup one: the company safe.

You deposit. Your money goes into the company's account and becomes a number on their screen. They hold it. They decide when it comes out. This is exactly like a normal online casino — the only difference is that you funded it with crypto instead of a credit card.

The upside is convenience and depth. They can offer you ten thousand games, because the money is all in one place and they can afford to run big promotions. The downside is that moment at the withdrawal screen, where a person you will never meet decides whether you get paid.

Setup two: the glass vault.

You deposit. Your money goes into a shared pot that sits on a public ledger — a blockchain — and is visible to anyone on earth at any moment. When you win, a piece of software releases the payout according to rules written into code you can read. No support agent, no "review," no manager who can say no.

The upside is that nobody can freeze your winnings without it being obvious to everyone. The downside is scale: these pots are often small, which means maximum bets are small, which means the experience can feel like a toy next to the big sites.

Here is the part that took me longest to accept: sites in the first group describe themselves using exactly the same words as sites in the second group. "Web3." "Provably fair." "Community-owned." I have now seen all three phrases on sites that hold your money in a company account, and on sites that don't. The labels are identical. The architecture is not. And the architecture is the thing that decides whether your $180 shows up.

Part Three: Five Questions That Decide Everything

This is the checklist I wish I'd had on night one. Learn these five and you can evaluate any site you land on, without reading a single whitepaper.

Question 1: Who is holding your money?

If it's the company, you have an account balance and a promise. If it's a piece of software on a public ledger, you have an actual position in a pot, and the payout rules are written into code you can read. The first is called custodial. The second is called non-custodial. That's the whole distinction. You don't need to know anything else to ask the question.

Question 2: Can anyone check the dice?

"Provably fair" is a real technique, not just a sticker. In a normal online casino, the dealer is software you can't see. Provable fairness lets you verify, after the round, that the outcome wasn't picked to make you lose.

The strongest version uses what's called a verifiable random function, or VRF — an outside service that produces a random number plus a mathematical proof it wasn't tampered with. A weaker version, called commit-reveal, works like a sealed envelope: the site publishes a scrambled fingerprint of its secret number before you bet, then reveals the secret after, so you can check that the fingerprint matches. Both methods work. Neither is a scam.

But here's the catch that nobody puts in the welcome email, and the one that genuinely annoyed me when I found it out: a site can be provably fair on five games and completely opaque on its other two thousand. Almost always, the provable part is the site's own in-house games, and the third-party slot machines — the ones I was actually playing on that first site — are not covered at all. I clicked the "verify" link on my dice game and it worked beautifully. Then I looked for the same thing on the slots and there was nothing there. The badge was on the homepage. The coverage was not.

Question 3: Who is paying the winners?

Every casino needs a pile of money to cover payouts. In a normal casino, that pile is the company's private treasury — nobody outside can see its size, and if it runs dry, your withdrawal waits.

In what's called DeFi — decentralized finance, which just means financial software that runs without a company in the middle — the pile is often a public pot that ordinary people deposit into, earning a share of the house's profits in exchange for taking on the risk of a lucky player. You can look up the pot's balance at any moment. If the pot is big, big bets get paid. If the pot is small, your max bet is small. And you can see that before you deposit, instead of discovering it at the withdrawal screen.

Question 4: Who reviewed the code?

An audit is an independent security review by a specialist firm. Several audits from respected firms is a good sign. It is not a guarantee. Audited code has been hacked before, and one site below is a case study in exactly that — three firms reviewed it and it still lost money.[16]

Question 5: Can the rules be changed after you deposit?

This is the one I never would have thought to ask. Some contracts have a maintenance hatch: the operator can rewrite the rules, pause transfers, or freeze funds after you've paid in. These are called upgradeable. Others cannot be changed by anyone, ever — immutable. Immutable is safer for you and less convenient for them. That tells you which one to want.

One term you'll see in the table: TVL, or total value locked — how much money is sitting in a site's public pot right now. Think of it as the casino's bankroll, except you're allowed to look at it. That single idea would have saved me a very annoying night.

Part Four: The Six That Keep the Money in Public View

These are the glass vaults. Note that they are not all equally good — one of them scores a 3 out of 10, and its lesson is important.

Overtime

Author's opinion score: 9/10 · True On-Chain DeFi dApp

The highest score here, and the easiest one to explain.

Overtime is a sportsbook. Here's what makes it different: when you bet on a team, the money to pay you if you win is already set aside at the moment you place the bet. It's not the company's promise to pay — it's a locked box that already contains the cash. The protocol is fully non-custodial, meaning users maintain control of funds at all times.[13] Settlement is trustless: when an event concludes, oracle data is written on-chain and winning positions are redeemed without any centralized operator involvement.[13]

Results come from Chainlink, a well-known independent data service that reports sports scores.[13] The bankroll is a public pot: when bettors win, it dips; when bettors lose, it refills. Over $200 million has been bet through it, across more than 50,000 users and 10,000+ markets.[1] The contracts are built on Thales's audited smart contract infrastructure.[0]

What this means for you: Your winnings don't depend on a support agent approving them. The money was there before the game ended.

Good

  • Payouts are pre-funded and software-released.
  • Built on audited smart-contract infrastructure.
  • Fully non-custodial settlement with no operator discretion.

Bad

  • The contracts can still be upgraded, so a hatch exists.
  • Results depend on an outside data provider.
  • Sportsbook only — no slots or table games.

Suigar

Author's opinion score: 8/10 · True On-Chain DeFi dApp

The cleverest randomness in this review, at the smallest scale.

Suigar's game outcomes come from the blockchain network itself — the same thousands of computers that keep the ledger honest also generate the random numbers, collectively. There's no outside vendor to trust and no operator who could secretly reroll. The network even has built-in protections that stop anyone from peeking at a random number and cancelling a losing bet.

Every penny of house profit goes to the people who put money in the public pot. The site itself takes zero: DefiLlama records revenue as $0, with all casino house edge paid to liquidity providers.[14] The code was audited by MoveBit in October 2025.[2] The SweetHouse bankroll holds about $183,000 at the time of writing.[14]

What this means for you: The dice cannot be rigged by the operator, because the operator doesn't control the dice.

Good

  • The most trustless randomness available.
  • 100% of profit to depositors; zero protocol revenue.
  • Public code, public audit.

Bad

  • The public pot holds about $183,000, which caps maximum bets severely.
  • Works on one blockchain network most people don't use.
  • A single audit firm rather than several.

SX Bet

Author's opinion score: 8/10 · True On-Chain DeFi dApp

Not a casino — a betting exchange, closer to a stock market.

You're not betting against the house. You're betting against another person. The site pairs you up and the money sits in escrow until the event resolves. All bets are executed and settled via publicly audited smart contracts, with no custodial intermediary holding user funds.[3] Because there's no house pot, there's no bankroll to inspect — that's why its published TVL reads $0, which sounds alarming but is simply what an exchange looks like. It has recorded over $200 million in cumulative volume.[15]

But the software that matches your bet to someone else's is closed-source — a black box nobody outside can inspect, so you rely on the team's own disclosures.[3]

What this means for you: When you win, you're paid by the person who lost, through software — not by a company deciding whether it feels like paying.

Good

  • Genuinely non-custodial.
  • Audited settlement contracts.
  • Low fees, no deposit or withdrawal charges.

Bad

  • The order-matching software is a black box.
  • Results depend on outside data feeds.
  • Thin markets can leave you without a counterparty.

JustBet / WINR

Author's opinion score: 7/10 · True On-Chain DeFi dApp

Strong foundations, with some accounting done off-stage.

The bankroll is a real public pot. Outcomes come from Supra, an outside service that produces verifiable random numbers.[4] All profit goes to the pot's depositors. It supports "smart wallets" that make each bet free of network charges, so it feels much like a normal casino.

The two catches: Your personal balance is tracked partly off the public ledger and reconciled later — the escrow holds user balances, settled on-chain via merkle roots.[4] That's better than a pure company account, but not fully in your control. And its published TVL currently reads $0, which is almost certainly a reporting glitch rather than an empty vault — but on a site about transparency, that's an unexplained gap.[4]

Good

  • Real public bankroll with profit-sharing.
  • Independent verifiable randomness via Supra.
  • Gas-free play via smart wallets.

Bad

  • Off-chain escrow bookkeeping for user balances.
  • TVL data is broken or alarming.
  • The in-house randomness tool hadn't finished auditing at review time.

Azuro

Author's opinion score: 6/10 · True On-Chain DeFi dApp

Good design, real security failure.

Azuro is largely plumbing that 30+ betting sites are built on top of. About $9.8 million sits in public pots, over $250 million has been wagered, and roughly $5 million in protocol revenue has been generated.[8] Three reputable firms have reviewed the code: Trail of Bits, Certora, and Diligence.[16]

But there was a documented bug in the payout routine — a reentrancy flaw, where paying one winner triggered code that re-entered and drained funds before the books updated — and it caused an actual loss of money.[16] CertiK separately notes that the protocol has not been audited by CertiK.[5]

What this means for you: This is the review's clearest lesson that audits are not immunity. Three firms looked at it and a real exploit still happened.

Good

  • Public pots with real depth.
  • Reputable auditors.
  • Shared liquidity across many apps.

Bad

  • A confirmed exploitable bug caused a real loss.
  • Not audited by CertiK; audit coverage is fragmented.
  • The token contract lacks a publicly submitted audit.

Degen Coin Flip

Author's opinion score: 3/10 · Hybrid Web3 Frontend

Proof that "on a blockchain" does not mean "safe."

The game runs on Base and the contract addresses are public.[6] But the contract has an owner who can pause transfers and can rewrite the contract — the maintenance hatch, wide open.

More seriously: nobody has published how the coin flip is decided. Not the odds, not the method, not the settlement rules. A confirmed exploit drained roughly $10,000 from the house, and the team confirmed the vulnerability while still withholding the function, randomness, odds, and settlement details.[6] No public audit report was located.

What this means for you: This is a blockchain casino in the same sense that a website with a shopping cart is an e-commerce company. The technology label is accurate and the protections are absent. You cannot verify what nobody will show you.

Good

  • Cheap network fees.
  • Contract addresses are publicly identifiable.
  • A documented token launch via Clanker.

Bad

  • No audit, undisclosed randomness, withheld exploit details.
  • An owner who can pause and rewire the game at will.
  • Extremely thin liquidity caps bets at trivial amounts.

Part Five: The Four That Keep It in the Company Safe

All four share one setup: you deposit, it becomes a balance on their screen, and their software decides whether you win. Where they claim provable fairness, it covers a handful of in-house games — not the thousands of third-party slots and live-dealer tables that make up most of what you'll actually play.

These are the sites I'd heard of before I started this. These are the ones with the ads.

Jack.com

Author's opinion score: 2/10 · Centralized Crypto Casino

The best of the four. The site says outright that it uses "custodial accounts and traditional game servers." Its in-house games are provably fair, and it earned an independent Gamecheck SEAL — an independent review confirming that a selection of its games are real and match the original providers' catalogues.[18] It holds a Curaçao Gaming Control Board license under OGL/2024/1800/1049.[9]

What this means for you: A legitimate, licensed, normal online casino that accepts crypto. Your money is in their account, and their word plus a seal is what stands between you and a frozen withdrawal. The seal only covers a selection of verified games — it does not verify every casino game, and it is not a substitute for provable fairness or a named RNG laboratory.[18]

Good

  • A real third-party verification seal.
  • Provable fairness on in-house games.
  • A regulatory license.

Bad

  • Custodial — the company holds your money.
  • Fairness stops at the third-party games; no named RNG lab.
  • Win caps exist but aren't published per game.

BC.Game

Author's opinion score: 2/10 · Centralized Crypto Casino

The company holds your money outright. The site does have a named RNG certification: an iTech Labs certificate covering its random number generator.[19] Some in-house games can be verified; the slots and live casino cannot. The treasury is private, and per-game payout caps aren't published.

What this means for you: Standard online casino, crypto deposits, and if a big win gets disputed you have no way to check the bankroll independently — even though the RNG itself has been tested.

Good

  • Huge game selection.
  • Many deposit options.
  • A named, independent RNG certificate (iTech Labs).

Bad

  • They hold the money outright.
  • Fairness does not extend to third-party slots and live games.
  • Nobody can see what backs the payouts.

Rollbit

Author's opinion score: 2/10 · Centralized Crypto Casino

In-house games use the sealed-envelope method — you can verify those rounds. But no audit of any kind is published, and the third-party slots and live games have no verification at all.[11] A deployer wallet has historically held roughly two-thirds of the token supply, an extraordinary concentration.[11] CertiK notes that the code is not open source and that major holders control over 81% of supply.[11] Separately, the co-founder has faced accusations of theft and rigged games, and users have reported withdrawal delays.

What this means for you: Good rakeback, generous VIP terms, and a company with a documented trust problem. The rakeback is real. So are the complaints.

Good

  • Verifiable commit-reveal on in-house games.
  • Generous rakeback.
  • A deflationary token model.

Bad

  • No audits at all.
  • Extreme token concentration in deployer and major-holder wallets.
  • Serious reputational red flags and withdrawal complaints.

Stake

Author's opinion score: 1/10 · Centralized Crypto Casino

The lowest score, because the risk isn't hypothetical.

In September 2023, roughly $41 million was drained from Stake's hot wallets in minutes.[7] A hot wallet is an internet-connected account holding the site's crypto — think of the safe kept in the lobby instead of the back room. That event is the clearest possible demonstration of what custody means: the balances were never the players' money. As one post-mortem put it, the games were verified perfectly on the day the signing service signed for a stranger.[17]

Stake's in-house Originals are provably fair with verifiable seeds. Stake itself has stated that third-party provider games are not covered — so the majority of the catalog runs on closed software nobody can check. The user experience, for what it's worth, is excellent.

What this means for you: The smoothest site in this review, with the weakest structural protection for your money — and a demonstrated loss to prove it.

Good

  • Best-in-class usability.
  • Verifiable in-house Originals.
  • Deep liquidity in normal operation.

Bad

  • Custodial, with a realized $41 million loss.
  • Fairness covers only a fraction of the catalog.
  • Zero visibility into the treasury.

Part Seven: The Three Things to Take Away

1. The difference is architecture, not marketing.

It's whether your money is in software anyone can inspect, or an account nobody can. Glass vault versus company safe. Everything else — the bonuses, the game counts, the rakeback percentages, the VIP hosts — is decoration on top of that single structural fact.

2. "On a blockchain" is not a safety certificate.

Degen Coin Flip is on a blockchain and scores 3/10, because the operator kept a pause switch and never explained the randomness.[6] The technology label tells you nothing. What tells you something is: do you keep control, can you verify the outcome, and did anyone independent check the code?

3. There's a trade-off nobody advertises.

The public pot that makes DeFi gambling transparent also makes it small. Suigar's randomness is the best in this review and its roughly $183,000 pot caps bets at levels a high roller would find insulting.[14] The big centralized casinos win on depth and convenience precisely because everyone's money sits in one place — which is also exactly why one morning in September can cost $41 million.[7]

So the choice isn't "safe versus unsafe." It's which risk you'd rather take:

A bug in the code, or a company holding your keys.

The first kind you can research before you deposit. The second kind you find out about at the withdrawal screen.

I found out the second kind the hard way, with $140 I'm never getting back. It was, in the end, a cheap lesson. I'd rather you got it for free.

Methodology

Each site was assessed on five criteria — who holds the money, how outcomes are generated and whether they can be verified, whether the bankroll is publicly visible, what independent security reviews exist, and how much friction the experience involves. Scores weight non-custodial execution, verifiable randomness, and transparent liquidity most heavily. Where something could not be independently confirmed — an unnamed auditor, an undisclosed random number method, a TVL reading of zero — it was counted against the site rather than assumed to be fine. Facts are cited to public sources; where no source exists for a negative claim (for example, "no audit was located"), that reflects the author's own search, not an assertion that no audit exists anywhere.

Glossary

Blockchain
A public ledger of transactions that can't easily be rewritten.
Smart contract
A program on a blockchain that holds and moves money by fixed rules. Think vending machine, not cashier.
Custodial / non-custodial
Whether the company holds your money, or you do.
Hot wallet
An internet-connected crypto account. Convenient, and the usual target of theft.
Provably fair
A method letting you verify an outcome wasn't rigged after the fact. Usually covers only a site's own games, not third-party slots.
VRF
A service producing a random number plus mathematical proof it's genuine.
Commit-reveal
Sealed-envelope method: publish a scrambled fingerprint of a secret beforehand, reveal it afterward so you can check.
Liquidity pool / pot
A shared pile of money backing payouts. In DeFi it's publicly visible, and its size sets the maximum bet.
TVL
Total value locked: how much is in that public pot right now.
Audit
An independent security review. Helpful, not a guarantee.
Upgradeable / immutable
Whether the operator can change the contract later, or nobody can, ever.
Gas fee
A small network charge for each transaction.
Reentrancy
A bug where a payout triggers code that re-enters and drains funds before the records update.
