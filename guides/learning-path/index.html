Module 0: What "crypto gambling" means

Crypto gambling sites differ mainly in who holds your money. That affects identity checks and what happens if the site stops paying out. The three setups below match the categories in the directory.

Custodial accounts

Platforms where you deposit crypto into an account the company controls.

  • Custody: the company holds your balance until you withdraw.
  • Identity: may apply. Terms can allow identity and source-of-funds checks, especially on large withdrawals.
  • If the site freezes withdrawals or stops operating: you depend on the company to pay you.
  • Directory: Large game libraries and Sports betting and casino. Many listings in these two categories describe a custodial account. Check the Custody field and status badge on each review.

Non-custodial and direct Web3 wallets

Platforms where you connect your own wallet and sign transactions that interact with a smart contract.

  • Custody: in a non-custodial design, your funds stay in your wallet until a bet executes.
  • Identity: usually no account sign-up. Check the KYC field on each review.
  • If the front-end goes down: you still need your own wallet and the correct network to interact with the contract.
  • Code risk: a bug in a contract can be exploited, and blockchain transactions generally cannot be reversed.
  • Directory: Wallet-connect platforms.

Lightning and instant payout specialists

Platforms that use Bitcoin Lightning or other low-fee networks for deposits and withdrawals.

  • Custody: varies. Some Lightning platforms hold balances on the platform, so the network does not change who holds the money.
  • Speed and fees: fast, low-fee settlement is the main difference from other networks.
  • Directory: Lightning and fast payouts.

For any category, ask three things: who holds the money, what identity checks apply and when, and what happens if the site stops responding. Module 1 turns these into a checklist.

Module 2: The play wallet setup

A play wallet is a separate wallet you use only for gambling, holding only the amount you are prepared to lose. Keeping it apart from savings limits what is exposed if something goes wrong.

The golden rule

Never deposit straight from an exchange. An exchange can freeze or restrict an account, and a deposit that depends on that account can stop there. A direct transfer also leaves a trail: it is visible on a public ledger, and the exchange holds records that identify the account that sent it. Move funds to your own wallet first, then send your play amount from there.

Create a dedicated play wallet

  1. Install a wallet from its official website or app store. Type the address yourself. Do not follow a link from an ad or a message.
  2. Create a new wallet. Do not reuse a wallet that holds savings.
  3. Write down the recovery phrase when the wallet shows it (see below).
  4. Move a small amount from your own wallet into the play wallet, on a network the platform supports (Module 3).
  5. Run the $1 test before any real deposit (Module 4).

Seed phrase safety

  • Write it on paper and keep one physical copy somewhere safe.
  • Never take a screenshot. Do not store it in notes, email or cloud storage.
  • Never share it. Anyone who asks for it is trying to take your funds. Support staff do not need it.
  • Never type it into a website, an app or a direct message, including messages from accounts claiming to be support.
  • There is no password reset. If the phrase is lost, the wallet cannot be recovered.

Why start with stablecoins

Stablecoins such as USDT and USDC aim to stay at $1. That means your balance does not swing with Bitcoin or Ether while you learn the process. They are not guaranteed to hold $1, and each carries its own issuer and network risks. Check which networks a platform supports before you choose one (Module 3).

Module 3: Networks and fees

A network is the blockchain a transaction travels on. The same token can exist on several networks, each with its own address format and fees. Fees vary and change with network congestion. The cheapest network is only useful if the platform supports it.

When each matters

  • TRC-20 matters when a platform lists USDT on Tron.
  • ERC-20 matters when you hold USDT or USDC on Ethereum. Fees can be high at busy times.
  • Solana matters when a platform lists tokens on Solana. Check the network name on the deposit screen, because the address has no fixed prefix.
  • Lightning matters for Lightning and fast payouts listings, where deposits and withdrawals are Bitcoin Lightning payments.

Same name, different network

"USDT on Tron" and "USDT on Ethereum" share a name, but they are different transfers. Sending one to an address on the other network often cannot be recovered.

The cheapest network is useless if the listing does not support it.

Module 4: The wrong network trap

A deposit on the wrong network is usually avoidable, and often not recoverable. The address is the first thing to check.

Reading a deposit address

The format is a clue, not proof. Use it to spot a mismatch.

  • Starts with T: Tron, used for TRC-20.
  • Starts with 0x: an EVM network, such as Ethereum for ERC-20. Several other networks use the same format, so 0x alone does not tell you which one.
  • Starts with bc1: a Bitcoin on-chain address.
  • Solana addresses have no fixed prefix. Rely on the network name shown on the deposit screen.

The network you choose on the platform's deposit page must match the network your wallet sends on. If they differ, stop.

The 30-second test

Send a small test amount, such as $1, before any real deposit. The test confirms that the address, network and wallet work together. It does not confirm that the platform supports that network for deposits, or that a withdrawal will work. If the test does not arrive, do not send more until you know why.

Generic flow

  1. Copy the deposit address from the platform's own deposit page. Do not copy addresses from chat messages, ads or direct messages.
  2. Paste it into your wallet's send screen.
  3. Check that the network in your wallet matches the network the platform shows.
  4. Send the $1 test.
  5. Check the transaction on a block explorer for that network before you send more.

Module 5: First deposit and first withdrawal

This walkthrough is generic and does not name any platform. Read the platform's own terms for limits, checks and timing.

  1. Get the deposit address and network. Select the network on the platform's deposit page first, then copy the address (Module 4).
  2. Send a test transaction. Send $1, as in Module 4.
  3. Check confirmations on a block explorer. Paste the transaction ID into the explorer for the network you used. Wait until it shows as confirmed. The platform may credit your balance only after a set number of confirmations.
  4. Understand withdrawals. A withdrawal is a request the platform processes before it sends funds to your address. Check the terms for minimum and maximum amounts, processing times and any verification step.
  5. Expect the first withdrawal to take longer. Some platforms run identity or account checks before the first payout. How long this takes depends on the platform.
  6. Return to stablecoin, then to an exchange. If you want fiat later, send the stablecoin from your wallet to an exchange, on a network that exchange supports. Test with a small amount first. The exchange may ask you to verify your identity.
